About me

Instructional design from a learner-centered perspective.

My background in education has given me direct experience observing how learners respond to content, where they struggle, and what kinds of activities help them move forward. My instructional design process

A loop, not a straight line.

Each stage feeds the next, and what the evidence shows at the end changes how I understand the need at the beginning.

01

Understand

Identify learner needs, learning difficulties, context, and existing evidence.

02

Define

Clarify what learners should know or be able to do.

03

Design

Choose activities, interaction, practice, and assessment.

04

Develop

Build the learning experience using appropriate tools.

05

Evaluate

Review learner evidence and reflect on improvements.

Evaluation returns to understanding — this is how I work, not a proprietary methodology.

Learners could name the problem, but struggled to reason about it aloud.

Learners showed difficulties with critical thinking and communicating their ideas when discussing environmental pollution.

Design decision

Move learners from private thinking to public reasoning.

The learning sequence was structured around Think-Talk-Write to move learners from individual thinking to collaborative discussion, written expression, and presentation.

Think Talk Write Share Reinforce Assess

Learning flow

Six stages of the sequence.

Open any stage to see what learners actually do.

Learners independently read, observe the pollution images on their worksheet, and make notes before any discussion begins.
Learners discuss ideas in heterogeneous groups, comparing what they noticed and filling gaps in each other's reasoning.
Groups communicate and compare their ideas, taking turns so every group contributes to the shared picture.
Learners independently write their understanding, turning the discussion into their own explanation.
The teacher clarifies concepts and addresses misconceptions before they settle into learners' written work.
Knowledge and communication skills are assessed against the learning outcomes for the topic.

Learning material

Image-based analysis tasks used in the sequence.

Learners analysed pollution images and explained the cause and the likely impact in their own words — the point where critical thinking and communication had to work together.

Assessment

What was assessed, and how.

Knowledge

Validated open-response items covering causes of pollution, impact analysis from images, and classification of pollutant types.

Communication skills

Group discussion, presentation of the group's reasoning, and individual written explanation after the discussion.

Average knowledge scores across the 22 learners moved from 53.86 to 83.55 in the documented classroom intervention. Individual scores are shown only in anonymised form.

What I learned

The tool came last, and that was the point.

The project reinforced the importance of designing around the learner's actual difficulty rather than starting with the tool. The digital format became a way to translate an existing instructional sequence into an interactive experience, rather than technology being the starting point.

Simple Past Tense — Find the Differences

English · Information gap activity · Storyboard

The learning objectives

What the storyboard sets out to achieve.

1

Use Simple Past Tense to describe completed events in a past-time context.

2

Ask and answer questions to obtain missing information.

3

Describe visual information without showing the partner's image.

4

Identify and report differences between two related pictures.

5

Speak with greater fluency and confidence during an information-gap task.

This is a storyboard and instructional design project. No learner score data is attached to it — the evidence here is the design itself.

The design challenge

Grammar practice that gives learners something to actually say.

The learning experience was designed to move grammar practice beyond isolated sentence exercises by placing Simple Past Tense in a purposeful communication task.

The design decision

Create a real reason to ask.

Student A and Student B receive different visual information. They cannot simply look at their partner's picture, so they must communicate by asking and answering questions to discover the differences.

See Ask Listen Describe Compare Report

Storyboard structure

Ten steps, from context to assessment.

Open any step to see what happens on screen and what learners do.

Introduce the real-world past/current visual context and activate past-time language.
Provide a minimal Simple Past grammar model and examples before the information-gap activity.
Create Student A / Student B information-gap conditions with different picture cards.
Learners ask purposeful questions to obtain the missing information.
Learners describe what they can see while listening carefully to their partner.
Learners record at least three differences supported by their conversation.
Learners report their findings to the class.
Review selected errors and improve language forms.
Learners reflect on what they can ask and what past-tense sentences they can produce.
Assess knowledge and speaking/communication performance against the stated objectives.

Interaction design

Partner information gap.

The information gap requires learners to communicate rather than simply observe both images.

Student A

Picture A. Student A describes what they can see and asks about Picture B.

Student B

Picture B. Student B describes their own picture and asks about Picture A.

Both pictures are shown here so the design can be reviewed. During the activity each learner sees only their own picture — the partner's image stays hidden, which is what creates the information gap.

Question prompts

Prompts from the storyboard that scaffold the exchange.

What did you see?
What happened?
Was the road damaged?
What about yours?

Communication assessment

Five assessment dimensions.

These are the dimensions the storyboard defines for observing speaking performance. They are not numerical results.

Fluency

Communicates intended meaning with reasonable continuity.

Questioning & responding

Asks for information and answers a partner appropriately.

Confidence

Participates willingly and speaks during the pair and reporting task.

Past-tense accuracy

Uses Simple Past Tense forms appropriately in the past-time context.

Cooperation

Listens, takes turns, and supports the partner.

From learning objective to storyboard

The design chain.

Learning objective
Use Simple Past Tense to ask, describe, and report in a past-time context.
Learning activity
A paired information-gap task built on two related pictures.
Learner interaction
Asking, listening, describing, and comparing without seeing the partner's image.
Assessment evidence
The completed difference log and the spoken report to the class.
